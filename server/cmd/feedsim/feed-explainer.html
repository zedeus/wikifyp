How the Feed Engine Works

The complete picture: what each mechanism does, what the current parameters are, and what 600 articles feel like for every type of reader. Updated after Round 14c (InterestFade shipped, E1-E3 and NascentBoost reverted).

The pipeline at a glance

Every time the app needs articles, it runs five stages. Each stage has a different job — and a different tradeoff.

1
Build the candidate pool
Take the reader's recent reads (last PoolWindow=200 state rows) and find every article that links to or shares categories with them. This is the raw material. A serial-rabbitholer who shifted interests 4 times doesn't need 500 rows of old cars polluting the pool — the window keeps it focused on where they're heading.
↓
2
Score every candidate
Each candidate gets a relevance score from shared links (worth 1.0 each) and shared categories (sqrt-saturated so volume can't dominate). Engagement weights the voice: finishing 95% of a 3000-word article counts far more than bouncing off a 60-word stub. InterestFade attenuates abandoned clusters — if you haven't read cars in 30 articles, the car cluster's voice drops to 30% of its peak. This sees the full state history, not just the pool window.
↓
3
Rotate across interest clusters
Your positive reads are grouped into clusters (category-cosine connected components). Each cluster gets rotation turns. The newest cluster gets 2× more turns than the oldest — so when you shift from cars to football, football ramps up fast while cars fades. A 5-interest juggler's clusters all get roughly equal turns because they're all recent.
↓
4
Mix in exploration
A fraction of the feed is doors you didn't ask for: featured Wikipedia articles, random corpus samples, and 2-3 hop random walks from your reads in the category graph. The share adapts: 75% for a brand-new reader (you have no pattern, so mostly explore), tapering to 20% once you have 30 clustered reads. If you're skipping a lot (>30% rate), exploration bumps up — the feed senses it's not working.
↓
5
Enforce diversity (MMR)
Even within relevance slots, candidates that are too similar to what's already picked get penalized (similarity penalty 0.6). This prevents 20 Lamborghini pages in a row. At 0.6, similar-but-different articles can coexist (Lamborghini Diablo and Toyota Corolla are both cars but different enough). At 1.0, even Toyota Corolla gets pushed down if Toyota Camry is already in the feed.

How scoring actually works — an example

You've read 8 articles. 6 are about cars, 2 are about music. Here's how a candidate article scores:

📊 Scoring "Lamborghini Gallardo"

This article links to 2 of your reads (Lamborghini Diablo, Porsche 911) and shares 4 categories with your car reads.

Link score: 2 links × 1.0 weight × voice (engagement × recency decay × InterestFade) = 1.4
Category score: sqrt(Σ liked[cat] × voice) across 4 shared cats = 0.8
Total relevance: 2.2 (well above floor of 0.3)

Now compare "Elvis Presley" — shares 2 music categories but no links. Score: 0.5. Still above floor, but the cluster rotation ensures music gets turns even though its total voice is lower.

The engagement signal

Not all reads are equal. The engagement weight comes from how much you read:

Strong positive
Read 95% of a 3000-word article
Weight: ~1.0 (full voice)
Moderate positive
Saved after reading 40%
Weight: ~0.7
Weak positive
Read 60% of a 200-word stub
Weight: ~0.4
Negative
Bounced after reading 5%
Weight: -0.8 (pushes categories down)

Engagement is normalized to the reader's personal mean — a reader who always reads 90% and one who always reads 50% are both "average" for themselves. Above-average reads boost; below-average reads dampen.

InterestFade — the newest mechanism

Before InterestFade, abandoned interests haunted the feed forever. The serial-rabbitholer would shift from cars → astronomy → football → history, and by phase 4, their history feeds were 67% old residue from the first three interests.

📉 How InterestFade works

For each interest cluster, compute what fraction of its members appear in the recent 50 state rows. Apply a fade multiplier:

fade = 0.3 + 0.7 × √(recentMembers / totalMembers)

A cluster with 20 total members and 0 in the recent window: fade = 0.3 (30% voice).

A cluster with 5 total members and 3 in the recent window: fade = 0.84 (84% voice).

A cluster with 10 members, all recent: fade = 1.0 (full voice).

The key insight: InterestFade applies to scoring (voice and nomination strength), not to selection (which clusters get rotation turns). The ClusterWindow already handles selection — old clusters fall off after 50 rows. InterestFade handles the subtler case: a cluster that still has rotation turns (recent enough) but whose engagement has waned. It's the difference between "you read cars recently" (cluster window) and "you used to read every car article, now you skip half of them" (interest fade).

The rotation system is built for this reader. Each of the 5 interest clusters gets equal turns regardless of how many corpus articles exist (there are 100× more music articles than animal articles). The recency boost doesn't distort things because all 5 interests are actively being read.

The interesting variance: Some seeds end up dominated by one interest — seed 3 has music 448 vs computing 6. This happens when the random walks from music happen to find more appealing articles than computing's walks. The rotation gives computing turns, but if computing's best candidate scores lower than the exploration threshold, it gets replaced by an exploration door. It's a feature, not a bug — if the corpus has more engaging music content, the reader naturally gravitates there.

😤 The Picky Reader

Who: Seeded with Lion and Penguin. Loves animals but only reads about 35% of what you show them — even within their interest. They skip freely.

53.2–65.0% appeal
17.0–25.0% repeats
0 quits
7–9 max boring streak
27–35% explore share

Why high repeats: Animals is a smaller corpus slice. After 600 articles at 35% pickiness, the reader has only deeply engaged with ~70 articles. The candidate pool runs thin — there aren't that many animal articles they haven't seen. The engine starts re-serving articles from slightly different angles (different category overlaps nominate the same page).

Why high explore share: Because picky readers skip so much, the skip-rate-reactive exploration kicks in — above 30% skip rate, the explore share increases. This is actually helpful: the relevance pool isn't serving what they want, so more exploration = more chances to find the specific animals they'd read.

The 53–65% range: This is the widest band of any persona. Some seeds happen to include more of the animals this reader likes (big mammals, ocean life) while others get dominated by insects and microscopic organisms they'd skip. It's the pickiness × corpus composition lottery.

🔀 The Shifter

Who: Started with cars (30 feeds), then switched hard to football (30 feeds). Seeds Messi and Bayern Munich at the transition.

62.8–79.2% appeal
6.8–10.8% repeats
0–7 quits
7–10 max boring streak

Phase 1 (cars): Consistently excellent — 72–83% active hit rate. The reader loves cars and the feed delivers.

Phase 2 (football): This is where it gets interesting. The best seed gets 76% active football, 10% residue cars. The worst seed gets 49% active, 26% residue. That spread is the transition penalty — how quickly the engine can pivot from 300 car reads to the new football signal.

What helps the transition: ClusterRecencyBoost (2×) gives football more rotation turns immediately. InterestFade drops the car cluster's voice as new state rows have no cars. ClusterWindow (50) means the car cluster eventually falls off rotation entirely. PoolWindow (200) keeps some car candidates in the pool (they still score, just lower), preventing a hard cliff.

The quits problem: Seeds 1 and 4 have 6–7 quits — feeds where the reader drew 10 articles and found nothing matching their current interest. These happen right at the transition: the first 2-3 feeds after switching, when football has barely any signal and cars still dominates. This is the worst moment in the reader's experience: "I just told you I like football now, why are you still showing me Lamborghinis?"

🆕 The Cold-Start Reader

Who: Brand new. No seeds, no history. Latent love of animals and computing — would devour either if they ever appeared.

66.5–70.5% appeal
4.3–5.8% repeats
0–1 quits
6–10 max boring streak

The surprise: Cold-start is actually one of the better personas now! 66–70% appeal with zero history. How?

Feed #1 is 75% exploration (no clustered reads yet). The mix of featured Wikipedia articles and random corpus samples almost always surfaces something the reader would love within the first feed. Computing is ~1% of the corpus but that's still 2,670 articles — random sampling hits it quickly. Once the reader finishes one computing article, the engine has a seed and starts finding neighbors.

The asymmetry: Computing gets found by feed 1 in every seed. Animals is slower (larger but more diffuse in the category graph). Some seeds find both; the worst seed gets computing early but never discovers animals. The 66–70% range reflects that: computing alone gets you to 66%, adding animals pushes toward 70%.

Adoption speed: Every seed adopted a latent interest at feed 1. The old system (featured articles only) took 3–4 feeds. The random-articles fix was the single biggest cold-start improvement.

🌊 The Scattered Reader

Who: Read 12 completely unrelated articles: Eiffel Tower, Photosynthesis, Jazz, Mount Everest, Sushi, Basketball, Leonardo da Vinci, Volcano, Bitcoin, Shakespeare, Great Barrier Reef, Chess. Would love astronomy and dogs if found.

50.5–61.3% appeal
5.8–12.8% repeats
5–10 quits
10 max boring streak
233–303 explore domains

The noise problem in detail: 12 random reads create 12 tiny clusters (or a few merged ones where categories happen to overlap — Chess + Leonardo da Vinci both share "Italian" categories). The engine treats each as a genuine interest. It rotates through chess neighbors, mountaineering neighbors, sushi neighbors — none of which the reader actually wants.

Dogs: never found. Dogs are 0.08% of the corpus (~215 articles). The random walks from the reader's 12 seeds never land on a dog breed. Pure random sampling draws from 267,000 articles — the chance of hitting a specific 215-article slice in 120 exploration slots over 600 articles is about 9%. Most seeds miss.

Astronomy: found by feed 3–6. Astronomy is ~1% of the corpus and connected to several of the reader's seeds (Volcano → Earth → Solar System, Mount Everest → Nepal → Himalayas → adjacent to space articles). The walks find it. Once found, it becomes the dominant interest and appeal climbs rapidly.

The 50–61% range: The floor (50.5%) is the seed where astronomy took until feed 6 to appear, and dogs were never found. The ceiling (61.3%) is the seed where astronomy appeared at feed 3 and the walks happened to find more useful adjacent content.

🤖 The Niche Explorer (gundam-dogs-fungi)

Who: Deep into mecha/anime. Also secretly loves dogs and fungi — two tiny corpus slices (dogs ~0.08%, fungi ~0.03%) that the engine can only find through exploration.

56.3–62.2% appeal
10.2–12.3% repeats
0–3 quits
6–10 max boring streak

Mecha dominates: 327–373 of 600 articles are mecha. The engine locked on immediately — 4 seeds with strong category overlap cluster fast.

Dogs: barely found. 1–15 dog articles across seeds. First contact varies wildly — feed 13 to feed 21. Some seeds find a few dog breeds through random exploration; most find 2–3 total. The corpus slice is just too small for exploration to reliably surface it.

Fungi: never found. Zero fungi articles across all seeds. At 0.03% of the corpus (~80 articles), random sampling essentially never hits it, and no category walk from mecha leads to mycology.

What this means: The engine is great at serving your dominant interest but structurally limited at discovering micro-niches. You can't find 80 articles out of 267,000 by random sampling in 600 draws. This is a real product limitation — a reader who would love mushroom articles will never know it exists unless they search for it themselves.

Random sampling might surface an animal article, but the reader's computing reads generate walks that lead to more computing. The engine found one interest and ran with it — great for depth, not great for breadth. This is the narrowing problem discussed in the struggles page.

How the engine evolved

Rounds 8–9 — Engagement model
Reading depth matters, not just "finished"
95% of a 3000-word article now counts far more than 95% of a 60-word stub. A 3000-word skip after 10 minutes of reading is a mild negative (−0.21), while an instant bounce is full negative (−1.0). Engagement is mean-normalized: your personal average is the baseline.
Round 10 — Scoring calibration
Saved ≠ max love; word-count-aware completions
Saving is moderate positive (~0.7), not maximum (1.0). Long articles (>3000w) complete at 45–85%, not 85–100%. Both make the sim more realistic.
Round 11 — The big parameter fix
Similarity 1.0→0.6, random articles for cold-start
Four flag-default bugs found and fixed. Similarity 0.6 was the single biggest win: picky +15.3, mono +13.7. Random articles for cold-start: +9.5 floor. Tradeoff: scattered and sparse regressed (noise signal gets more trust).
Round 12 — Selection structure
Cluster window, pool window, recency boost
ClusterWindow=50 limits old interests in rotation. PoolWindow=200 keeps pool focused on recent interests. RecencyBoost=2× accelerates transitions. Serial phase 4 went from 2% to 35–47% active.
Round 14 — InterestFade
Per-cluster engagement trending for scoring
Clusters that stopped appearing in recent state get voice attenuated to 30%. Works on both voice (category weight) and nominations (link strength). The scoring-side complement to ClusterWindow's selection-side windowing.
Round 14b–c — Activity-ratio experiments (reverted)
E1-E3, NascentBoost: all failed
Tried applying activity ratio to selection mechanisms (explore share, cluster boost, fresh cap). Every variant hurt at least one persona. Also tried NascentBoost (voice amplification for small new clusters) — helped shifter +10 but hurt others because it can't distinguish real interest transitions from exploration noise. Conclusion: the system is at its Pareto frontier with InterestFade alone.

The fundamental tradeoffs

⚖️ Trust vs. Explore

More trust in the signal → better for readers with clear interests (cars, mono), worse for noisy signals (scattered, serial). Less trust → more exploration → helps noisy readers discover things, but dilutes focused readers' feeds with unwanted doors.

Current config: trust the signal, with a safety net. Similarity 0.6, base exploration 20%, adaptive ramp from 75% to 20%, skip-reactive boost.

⚖️ Speed vs. Stability

Faster transitions help serial and shifter (the engine pivots to new interests quickly). But faster transitions also mean the engine overreacts to exploration noise — a random astronomy read creates a cluster that competes with established interests.

Current config: medium speed. RecencyBoost 2×, ClusterWindow 50, InterestFade floor 0.3. An abandoned interest fades but doesn't vanish — enough residual voice to serve its best candidates if they appear, not enough to dominate.

⚖️ Depth vs. Breadth

Depth (more similar articles within an interest) helps focused readers who want to go deep into Italian supercars. Breadth (diverse articles, more exploration) helps readers discover new things. Similarity penalty is the main dial: at 0.6 depth wins; at 1.0 breadth wins.

Current config: slightly favoring depth. 0.6 similarity penalty lets similar articles coexist. The 20% exploration floor ensures breadth never drops below one-fifth of the feed.
