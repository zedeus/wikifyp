When the Feed Struggles

Five real failure modes, what the reader actually sees, and why the engine can't do better yet. From the 6-seed benchmark (267K articles, 600 reads each).

🌊 The Noise Problem

Persona: Scattered — 12 unrelated reads (Eiffel Tower, Chess, Bitcoin, Volcano…). Would love astronomy and dogs.

The problem: The engine reads 12 random articles and tries to build a profile from noise. Each seed creates a mini-cluster of "related" articles, and the engine faithfully serves their neighbors. But chess → chess tournaments and Everest → mountaineering aren't what the reader wants.

Why it's fundamentally hard

The core issue: The engine can't distinguish "the reader read 12 random things because they're browsing casually" from "the reader has 12 genuine interests." Both look identical from the engagement data. The engine does the reasonable thing — treats each read as a signal and serves neighbors. But for this reader, every signal is noise.

What helps: The explore share is elevated (22–33%) because the reader's clustered rows are few and skip rate is high. That's the lifeline — more random sampling = more chances to stumble onto astronomy. Once found, the real interest rapidly dominates because the reader engages deeply with every astronomy article.

What doesn't help: Dogs (0.08% of corpus). Pure random sampling hits ~215 articles out of 267K. In 600 total articles at 25% explore, that's ~150 random draws. Probability of hitting at least one dog breed: ~9%. Most seeds miss it entirely. This isn't fixable without either a larger explore share (which hurts every other persona) or a different discovery mechanism entirely.

🔀 The Transition Penalty

Persona: Shifter — 30 feeds of cars, then seeds Messi and switches hard to football for 30 feeds.

The problem: When the reader switches, the engine has 300 car reads and 2 football seeds. It takes time for football to build enough signal to compete — and during that time, the reader sees cars they no longer want.

The mechanics of residue

InterestFade fades, it doesn't erase. A cluster with 150 members and 0 in the recent 50 rows gets fade = 0.3. But 0.3 × 150 members means the cluster still has effective voice equivalent to 45 active members. Compare that to the history cluster's 5 members at full voice (5.0). The old cluster outweighs the new one 9:1 in absolute voice.

Why not fade to 0? Because it would hurt the juggler. A juggler's fifth interest (computing, 6 reads across 60 feeds) would get zeroed out the moment a couple of feeds passed without a computing article. The 0.3 floor keeps sparse-but-real interests alive. The cost: massive old clusters also stay alive.

ClusterWindow helps selection but not scoring. The car cluster falls off rotation after 50 rows (it no longer gets turns). But its categories still have voice in scoring — a history article that shares a category with a car article (e.g., "United States" or "Manufacturing") gets a small boost from the dead car cluster's residual voice. That boost is enough to beat the relevance floor (0.3).

🔬 The Invisible Interest

Personas: Adopter (cars + latent astronomy), Gundam (mecha + latent dogs + latent fungi)

The problem: The reader has a latent interest the engine doesn't know about. Discovery depends entirely on exploration doors randomly surfacing the right article. When the interest is a tiny corpus slice, the odds are against you.

The adopter: when discovery happens (and when it doesn't)

When did the adopter discover astronomy? (6 seeds)
Seeds 2 and 4 discovered astronomy at feed 4 — a random exploration door happened to serve Jupiter or a space article very early. These seeds end with 307–337 astronomy articles (over half their feed!) because once discovered, astronomy competes with cars and the reader loves both.

Seeds 1 and 3 didn't find astronomy until feed 38. That's 380 articles of pure cars before a single space article appeared. These seeds end with only 14-15 astronomy articles. The reader never got enough astronomy signal to build a strong cluster.

Gundam: the micro-niche problem

Dogs (0.08% of corpus, ~215 articles): Found in 5 of 6 seeds, but only 1–15 articles each. First contact: feed 13–21. The random walks from mecha never lead to dog breeds (no category overlap). Discovery is pure random sampling luck.

Fungi (0.03% of corpus, ~80 articles): Never found. Zero articles across all 6 seeds. At 80/267,000 = 0.03%, random sampling in ~120 exploration slots has a probability of about 3.5% per seed. All 6 seeds missed. This is a structural limitation of corpus-random exploration for micro-niches.

Why exploration can't fix this

The explore share is 20% (established reader). Over 600 articles, that's ~120 exploration slots. Each slot draws from:

  • Random walks (2-3 hops): Follow links and categories from your reads. For a car reader, walks lead to racing, engines, manufacturers — never dogs.
  • Featured articles: Wikipedia's curated set. ~8% animals, ~5% computing, ~0% fungi.
  • Random corpus samples: Pure lottery. P(dog breed) ≈ 0.08% per draw.

The walks are the most effective but the most narrow. Featured articles are higher quality but biased. Random sampling is the only unbiased discovery mechanism, but the probability math is against micro-niches.

The broader point: Any corpus-based recommendation system has this problem. TikTok, YouTube, Spotify — they all rely on some form of collaborative filtering (other people who like X also liked Y) to bridge the gap. We don't have that signal. Our only bridge between "mecha fan" and "loves mushrooms" is random chance. This is arguably the biggest structural gap in the system.

📏 The Narrowing Feed

Personas: Cars (established), mono-football, history-broad

The problem: For readers with a strong single interest, the feed does its job too well. 80% of articles are on-topic. The 20% exploration exists, but it's only 2 articles per 10. The reader is in a pleasant bubble — they're happy, but they'll never discover something unexpected.

What 80% appeal actually looks like over time

This is the cars reader's 600 articles. Every dot is one article:

The feed is consistently green — 8 of 10 articles are cars, reliably, from feed 1 to feed 60. There's no surprise, no variation in tone, no moment where the reader thinks "whoa, I didn't expect that." The few gray dots are exploration doors, and many of those land on car-adjacent things (racing, engines) anyway.

The exploration that does happen

180–236 explore domains
317–375 explore categories

The 180–236 explore domains sounds diverse — the reader saw articles from 200+ different Wikipedia categories. But seeing ≠ engaging. The reader finished maybe 5 of those 120 exploration articles (because they only like cars). Those 5 were probably car-adjacent things that reinforced the existing interest rather than opening a new one.

Is this actually a problem?

For the reader: probably not. They love cars. They're getting cars. Appeal is 80%. They're happy.

For the product: maybe. A feed that's 80% one thing is a reference tool, not a discovery engine. The reader could get the same experience by searching "cars" on Wikipedia. The product's value proposition is "an endless feed that helps you discover things you didn't know you'd love." For this reader, we're delivering the first half (endless feed of things you love) but failing at the second half (discover things you didn't know).

The uncomfortable question: Would this reader be better served with a higher exploration share? At 30% explore, they'd see 3 non-car articles per feed instead of 2. Their appeal would drop from 80% to maybe 70%. But they'd be 50% more likely to stumble onto that latent astronomy interest. Is 70% appeal with discovery potential better than 80% appeal in a bubble?

What the research says

According to a 2024 study of TikTok's algorithm, TikTok shows personalized content (exploitation) in only 30–50% of recommended videos. The other 50–70% is exploration. That's two to three times our exploration share for established readers.

This might explain why TikTok feels more "surprising" even though it clearly knows your interests. It's not that TikTok doesn't know you like cooking — it's that it deliberately fills most of your feed with things you might like, from adjacent topics, trending content, and cold-start items it wants to test on you.

The tradeoff is real though. TikTok has collaborative filtering — "people who like car videos also like X" — which means its exploration is informed. Our exploration is either random walks (narrow, follows the interest graph) or pure random sampling (unbiased but uninformed). Higher exploration share with uninformed exploration just means more noise. We'd need smarter exploration, not just more of it.
