How Our Feed Compares to Doomscrolling Platforms

TikTok, YouTube Shorts, Instagram Reels — they do show you things you're interested in, but they feel fundamentally different from our feed. This page explores why, what they know that we don't, and whether we're measuring the right things.

The key insight: TikTok's exploration is informed

You said it perfectly: TikTok doesn't just show "90% cars, 5% car-adjacent, 5% random." It shows you things you'll find fun, funny, emotionally impactful, surprising. The reason it can do this is a capability we fundamentally lack:

🧠 Collaborative Filtering

What it is: "People who like the same things you like also liked X." If 10,000 users who watch car videos also watch cooking fails, TikTok knows to show you cooking fails — even though cars and cooking share zero content overlap.

Why it matters: This is the bridge between "you like cars" and "you'd find this hilarious cooking disaster entertaining." No amount of category-graph walking from car articles leads to cooking. But human interest patterns do — people who like one thing often like another seemingly unrelated thing, and collaborative filtering captures that.

What we have instead: Category graph walks (narrow — they follow Wikipedia's editorial links) and random corpus sampling (unbiased but uninformed). Our exploration doors are random lottery tickets. TikTok's exploration doors are educated guesses based on billions of data points.

📏 What "appeal" misses

Surprise: Our picky reader gets 53–65% appeal. But if all 65% are "Lion," "Tiger," "Elephant" — it's boring even at 65%. A feed with 50% appeal but including "The biomechanics of how cheetahs run" and "Why zoos are redesigning enclosures" would feel better. The surprise makes the difference. Research calls this serendipity — "content that feels fortuitous, refreshing, and enriching."

Emotional range: TikTok deliberately varies emotional tone — a funny video, then something awe-inspiring, then something heartwarming. Research shows that emotional homogeneity ("the same mood, repeated") is a key driver of feed fatigue, even when the content is topically relevant. Our benchmark can't distinguish between 10 dry car specification articles and a mix of "the engineering genius of the Porsche 911" + "a road trip across Route 66" + "the history of Le Mans."

Tangential pull: The YouTube Shorts experience isn't "here's cars." It's "here's a satisfying engine restoration → here's someone racing go-karts → here's a road trip timelapse → here's a cool gadget review." Each video is adjacent to but different from the previous one. Our feed serves "Lamborghini Gallardo → Lamborghini Urraco → Lamborghini Diablo" — topically correct but experientially flat.

What the research says about exploration ratios

📊 TikTok: 50–70% exploration

A 2024 WWW paper by Vombatkere et al. (Boston University / University of Washington) analyzed 220 real TikTok users' recommendation data and found that TikTok's algorithm only "exploits" (shows personalized content matching known interests) in 30–50% of recommendations. The rest is exploration.

But here's the crucial difference: TikTok's exploration is informed by collaborative filtering. A "random" video on your TikTok FYP isn't actually random — it's something the algorithm is testing on you, based on what people with similar taste profiles engaged with. When it works, the user thinks: "wow, how did it know I'd like this?" When our random exploration works, the user thinks: "huh, weird, an asteroid article — but okay, that was interesting."

🎵 Spotify: familiarity is winning

Spotify's Discover Weekly launched as a novelty-focused playlist (30 songs you've never heard). But over the past two years, Spotify found that listeners stay longer when they hear music they already have a relationship with. The algorithm now weights familiarity and proven engagement more heavily than raw novelty.

This is interesting because it suggests the optimal ratio isn't fixed — it depends on the medium. Short video (TikTok) benefits from high exploration because each item costs 15 seconds to evaluate. Long-form reading (our app) costs minutes per article. The cost of a bad exploration recommendation is higher for us.

🔬 Serendipity research

A 2025 paper on serendipitous recommendation proposes estimating "user curiosity" — long-term and short-term interest signals — to balance usefulness vs. unexpectedness. The key insight: surprise alone isn't valuable. Serendipity is surprise that the user values. An asteroid article for a car reader is surprising but not serendipitous. A "road trip across Route 66" article for a car reader is both surprising and serendipitous — it connects to their interest from an unexpected angle.

A 2025 IEEE paper uses knowledge graphs to find items that are "semantically related yet sufficiently distant" — a two-hop distance approach. This is conceptually similar to our random walks, but with deeper semantic understanding of what "related but different" means.

What this means for our test suite

Our 14-persona benchmark measures appeal, repeats, quits, boring streaks, and explore domains. These are necessary but not sufficient. Here's what we're missing and what we might add:

🔍 What we measure well

Appeal: "Would this reader want to read this?" — our core metric. Measures basic feed quality.

Quits: "Was there a feed with zero hits?" — measures the worst moments.

Boring streaks: "How many misses in a row?" — measures the longest pain.

Explore domains: "How many different Wikipedia categories did exploration touch?" — a rough breadth measure.

🚫 What we can't easily measure

Serendipity: "Was the reader surprised and delighted by something unexpected?" Our sim can't model delight — it either matches the interest or it doesn't. A real reader might love "History of Le Mans" even though it's categorized as sports/history, not cars.

Emotional variety: We'd need some kind of article-level emotional tagging (fascinating vs. dry vs. narrative vs. listicle). Wikipedia's category system doesn't encode this.

Tangential discovery: "Did the reader find something adjacent to their interest that opened a new thread?" We could measure this with the adopter persona (latent discovery), but only for the specific latent interests we hardcode.

💡 What we could add

Category diversity within hits: Right now "cars 480" means 480 car articles, but we don't track whether those were 480 sedan specs or a mix of racing history, engineering, culture, road trips, and design. We could measure the number of distinct sub-categories within the hit set. A wider spread = more tangential pulls within the interest.

Exploration conversion rate: Of the ~120 exploration doors over 600 articles, how many did the reader actually finish? A high conversion means exploration is finding things the reader likes. A low conversion means random noise. Right now we report explore share but not explore quality.

Interest width: How many distinct interest clusters does the reader end up with? A cars reader who ends with 1 cluster is narrowing. One who ends with 3 (cars + engineering + travel) discovered tangential interests through the feed.

Time-to-discovery for latent interests: The adopter persona already tracks "adopted latent at feed N." We could add more latent interests to other personas (e.g., give the cars reader a latent love of engineering-adjacent things) and measure how quickly the feed finds them.

The honest comparison

Here's the uncomfortable truth:

Our feed is a Wikipedia-powered topic matcher, not a recommendation engine. We find articles related to what you've read, enforce some diversity, and throw in random exploration doors. TikTok is a multi-signal recommendation engine with billions of users, deep content understanding, collaborative filtering, and emotional modeling. They're playing different games.

But that's not necessarily bad. The product isn't trying to be TikTok. It's trying to be a doomscrolling replacement for curious people — the key word being "replacement." The value proposition is:

"Instead of scrolling TikTok for 45 minutes and feeling empty, scroll this for 45 minutes and learn something."

The feed doesn't need to be as addictive as TikTok. It needs to be addictive enough to compete for attention, while delivering on the "learn something" promise.

Where to go from here

Given these constraints (single user, no social graph, Wikipedia content), what could move the needle?

1. Smarter exploration (feasible)

What: Instead of random corpus samples, use article embeddings (precomputed from article text via a sentence-transformer) to find articles that are semantically related but categorically different. A car reader would get "the engineering of highway interchanges" or "how Formula 1 aerodynamics work" instead of "Jeremy Corbyn."

Why it helps: This is the closest we can get to TikTok's informed exploration without collaborative filtering. The exploration doors would be tangential-by-design rather than random-and-hoping.

Cost: Requires precomputing embeddings for 267K articles and a nearest-neighbor index. Non-trivial but doable.

2. Article quality signal (feasible)

What: Not all Wikipedia articles are equally engaging. A 200-word stub about a minor car model is far less compelling than a 5000-word featured article about the history of Porsche. Use article length, featured/good article status, and link density as a quality prior in scoring.

Why it helps: Better articles → higher completion → stronger signal → faster interest learning. Also improves the subjective experience: the reader gets fewer "huh, a 3-sentence article about a car I've never heard of" moments.

Cost: Low. The data already exists (word count, featured status). Just needs to be factored into scoring.

3. Sub-interest diversity (feasible)

What: Within a single interest cluster (cars), track sub-topics (racing, engineering, culture, manufacturers) and rotate across them. A car feed that's 30% racing history + 30% engineering + 20% culture + 20% model specs would feel more varied than 80% model specs.

Why it helps: This is the "tangential pull" that TikTok does well. You don't just like "cars" — you like car engineering, car culture, car history, and car racing. Diversifying within the interest mimics the variety that doomscroll feeds provide within a topic.

Cost: Medium. Would need a sub-clustering step within each interest cluster. Wikipedia's category hierarchy provides the structure.

4. Collaborative filtering (hard, requires users)

What: Track what articles readers finish, build a co-engagement matrix, use it to bridge between unrelated interests.

Why it helps: This is the missing piece. It's what makes TikTok's exploration feel magical.

Cost: Requires many users. Not feasible at current scale. But worth building the infrastructure for (event collection, anonymous similarity) so it's ready when user numbers grow.

So: what should we optimize for?

The honest answer: appeal is the right primary metric, but it's not enough alone.

A feed with 80% appeal and zero surprise is a search engine in feed clothing. A feed with 50% appeal and 30% surprise is TikTok for learning. The sweet spot is probably somewhere around 65% appeal + meaningful tangential discovery — most articles hit your interest, but every 4th or 5th one is a pleasant surprise that either deepens your understanding or opens a new thread.

Our current system optimizes purely for appeal. The test suite's definition of "good" is binary: either the article matches the persona's predefined interests or it doesn't. There's no credit for "this wasn't about cars, but a car lover would find it fascinating." There's no credit for variety within an interest. There's no penalty for 10 identical Lamborghini specs in a row if the reader "likes cars."

The meta-insight: The benchmark optimizes for what it can measure. It can measure topical match. It can't measure delight, surprise, or tangential fascination. So every optimization we do pushes toward more topical match and less surprise — the opposite of what makes a feed feel alive. We might be Goodharting ourselves: the metric improves but the experience flatlines.

The next step isn't a code change. It's expanding what we measure — so we can optimize for a feed that's not just accurate, but interesting.
